Financial Trend Analysis: Microsoft, Tesla, and Apple (FY2023–FY2025)¶

Purpose: extract and analyze key financial metrics from the 10-K filings of Microsoft, Tesla, and Apple for fiscal years 2023–2025, to identify trends that could inform the design of an AI-powered financial chatbot (e.g. what questions it should be able to answer, and what it needs to know to answer them correctly).

Data source: all figures were extracted manually from each company's Form 10-K, as filed with the SEC's EDGAR database (sec.gov/edgar), and compiled into financial_data.xlsx / financial_data.csv. See the Notes sheet in the Excel file for exact filing dates, CIK numbers, and metric definitions.

A methodology note before we start: the three companies do not share a fiscal calendar — Microsoft's fiscal year ends June 30, Apple's ends on the last Saturday of September, and Tesla's follows the standard calendar year (ending December 31). "FY2025" therefore refers to three different 12-month windows. This doesn't affect each company's own year-over-year trend, but it means a same-fiscal-year comparison across companies is an approximation, not a same-quarter comparison — worth keeping in mind (and worth an AI chatbot being able to explain) when someone asks "how did Apple compare to Tesla in 2025?".

2. Year-over-year growth¶

For each company, we calculate the year-over-year percentage change in Total Revenue and Net Income. Sorting by Company and Fiscal Year first ensures pct_change() compares each year to the previous year within the same company, not across companies.

Reading the growth table:

  • Microsoft grew revenue and net income steadily and accelerated in FY2025 (+14.9% revenue, +15.5% net income), consistent with cloud/AI-driven demand.
  • Apple grew slowly in FY2024 (+2.0% revenue) with net income actually down slightly (mix/tax effects), then re-accelerated sharply in FY2025 (+6.4% revenue, +19.5% net income).
  • Tesla is the outlier: strong FY2023, essentially flat revenue in FY2024 (+0.9%), and revenue declined in FY2025 (-2.9%) while net income collapsed both years (-52.4% in FY2024, then -46.8% in FY2025) — profit fell far faster than revenue, which points to margin compression rather than a demand problem alone.

3. Additional derived metrics¶

Revenue and net income growth tell only part of the story. We add:

  • Net Margin (%) = Net Income ÷ Total Revenue — how much of every revenue dollar becomes profit.
  • Liab/Assets (Liabilities-to-Assets Ratio) = Total Liabilities ÷ Total Assets — a simple leverage / balance-sheet-risk indicator.
  • Op CF / Net Income (Operating Cash Flow to Net Income Ratio) = a rough "earnings quality" check — cash generated per dollar of reported profit. A ratio well above 1 (Tesla, and Microsoft in the recent years) suggests income including large non-cash charges (e.g. depreciation on the AI/datacenter buildout); a ratio near or below 1 is more typical for a mature software/hardware business's steady state.
  • Asset Growth (%) and Liabilities Growth (%) — year-over-year, same pattern as the revenue/income growth above.

df["Net Margin (%)"] = (
    df["Net Income ($M)"] / df["Total Revenue ($M)"] * 100
)
df["Liab/Assets"] = (
    df["Total Liabilities ($M)"] / df["Total Assets ($M)"]
)
df["Op CF / Net Income"] = (
    df["Operating Cash Flow ($M)"] / df["Net Income ($M)"]
)
df["Asset Growth (%)"] = (
    df.groupby("Company")["Total Assets ($M)"].pct_change() * 100
)
df["Liabilities Growth (%)"] = (
    df.groupby("Company")["Total Liabilities ($M)"].pct_change() * 100
)

Reading the charts:

  • The revenue chart shows Apple and Microsoft on clearly rising paths, with Microsoft's slope steepening in FY2025 — while Tesla's line is essentially flat and turns down in FY2025.
  • The net income chart is more dramatic than the revenue chart for Tesla: a steep decline across both years, while Microsoft and Apple's net income keeps pace with (or, for Apple in FY2025, outpaces) revenue.
  • Net margin makes Tesla's story explicit: margin roughly halved in FY2024 and roughly halved again in FY2025, while Microsoft's and Apple's margins stayed in a fairly narrow band the whole period.
  • The leverage ratio shows Apple carrying by far the highest liabilities-to-assets ratio of the three (Apple funds itself heavily with debt and payables relative to its asset base, partly reflecting large share buybacks), Microsoft's has drifted up slightly as it has taken on debt to fund AI infrastructure, and Tesla's is the lowest and most stable of the three.
  • The FY2025 growth bar chart puts the divergence side-by-side: Microsoft and Apple grew on both axes; Tesla shrank on both.

6. Summary of findings¶

Microsoft (FY2023–FY2025): Consistent, accelerating growth. Revenue grew from $211.9B to $281.7B (+33% over two years) and net income from $72.4B to $101.8B, with net margin holding in the mid-to-high 30s. Total assets grew faster than revenue (+50% vs. +33%), and the liabilities-to-assets ratio ticked up modestly — consistent with the public reporting on Microsoft's heavy capital investment in AI/datacenter capacity, funded partly with new debt.

Apple (FY2023–FY2025): A near-flat FY2024 (revenue +2.0%, net income essentially flat) followed by a strong FY2025 (revenue +6.4%, net income +19.5%, aided partly by services growth and a favorable prior-year tax comparison). Apple's balance sheet is comparatively asset-light with the highest liabilities-to-assets ratio of the three, reflecting its large buyback-funded capital structure rather than distress — its net margins stayed healthy (25–27%) throughout.

Tesla (FY2023–FY2025): The clearest inflection point in the dataset. Revenue peaked in FY2024 and declined in FY2025, while net income fell in both years — down more than 70% cumulatively from FY2023's $15.0B to FY2025's $3.8B. Net margin compressed from 15.5% to 4.0%. Notably, operating cash flow held up much better than net income (~$13–15B every year), meaning the profit decline is concentrated in pricing and margin pressure rather than a collapse in the underlying cash-generating business — an important nuance a chatbot summarizing "Tesla's profits fell 75%" should be able to surface rather than leaving out.

Cross-company takeaway: by FY2025, Microsoft and Apple converged on strong, growing, high-margin profiles from very different starting points (services/cloud vs. hardware/services), while Tesla — despite having similar-scale revenue to the other two in FY2023 — diverged sharply downward on profitability even as its balance sheet kept growing (total assets +29% from FY2023 to FY2025). Revenue size alone would miss this; it takes margin and cash-flow context together to see it.

7. Implications for an AI-powered financial chatbot¶

This analysis suggests a few concrete requirements for a chatbot built to answer questions about company financials like these:

  1. Never report a single metric in isolation. "Tesla's net income fell 47% in FY2025" is technically correct but misleading without also surfacing that operating cash flow was roughly flat — the chatbot should default to pairing profitability metrics with a cash-flow or margin cross-check, especially when the two diverge.
  2. Fiscal-year alignment needs to be explicit. Since these three companies don't share a fiscal calendar, a chatbot comparing "FY2025" across companies should state each company's actual period-end date, not just the FY label, to avoid implying a same-quarter comparison that isn't there.
  3. Growth rates need a "growth from what base" caveat. Tesla's FY2023 net income growth was healthy off a smaller earlier base; Microsoft and Apple's FY2025 acceleration is happening off an already-large base. A chatbot should be able to contextualize a growth percentage against the underlying dollar figures, not just recite the percentage.
  4. Leverage ratios need business-context framing. Apple's higher liabilities-to-assets ratio isn't a distress signal — it reflects a deliberate, buyback-heavy capital structure. A chatbot that flags "high leverage" without that context risks giving a misleadingly alarming answer.
  5. The underlying structured data (this table) is what makes those answers possible. A chatbot answering from unstructured 10-K text alone would struggle to compute year-over-year growth or cross-company ratios reliably; keeping a clean, versioned table like financial_data.csv — with fiscal year, period-end date, and source filing noted per row — as the chatbot's underlying data source is what lets it calculate (rather than guess at) these figures on demand.
